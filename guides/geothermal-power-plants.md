# Geothermal power plants: how they work, the three types, and what they produce.

A geothermal power plant brings hot water or steam up through wells, uses it to spin a turbine, and sends the cooled water back underground. There are three types: dry steam plants use steam straight from the ground, flash plants boil high-pressure hot water into steam, and binary plants pass the heat to a second fluid that boils at a lower temperature. The world had 17,173 MW of geothermal power capacity at the end of 2025, and the US fleet ran at a 65.9% capacity factor.

Plant types and figures are quoted from the EIA, the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR, formerly NREL), ThinkGeoEnergy and Calpine. Nevada plant output is measured and operator-reported to the Nevada Division of Minerals. Work-per-kilogram figures are Zemanium model results, labelled [MODEL].

01 / Mechanism

## How does a geothermal power plant work?

![Diagram of how a geothermal power plant works: a production well brings hot water and steam from the reservoir, a separator splits off the steam, the steam spins a turbine and generator, a condenser and cooling tower turn it back into water, and an injection well returns the water to the reservoir](https://zemanium.com/assets/charts/geothermal-power-plant-diagram.svg)

A flash geothermal power plant, the most common type. Binary plants swap the separator for a heat exchanger and a second working fluid. Not to scale. Free to reuse under CC BY 4.0 with credit to Zemanium.

1. Production wells reach a reservoir of hot water or steam, or hot rock that engineers have made permeable. The EIA notes that plants use water or steam at 300 to 700 °F and that some wells go as deep as about 2 miles.
2. The fluid rises to the surface under its own pressure or with a downhole pump.
3. Its heat drives a turbine: as steam directly, as steam flashed from hot water, or through a heat exchanger that boils a second fluid.
4. The turbine turns a generator, which feeds the grid.
5. The cooled water goes back down injection wells, keeping reservoir pressure up and returning the fluid to be heated again.

Nothing burns, and the heat does not depend on weather, so a geothermal plant runs day and night. In 2025 the US fleet produced 65.9% of what its available capacity could deliver running flat out, against 24.0% for solar and 33.8% for wind, by the EIA's count.

### How is geothermal energy produced?

Geothermal energy comes from deep inside the Earth, produced by the slow breakdown of radioactive particles, a process that happens naturally in all rocks, by the EIA's account. The heat flows up through the crust, so the deeper you go, the hotter the rock. Where it meets water in permeable rock it forms a reservoir of hot water or steam, and a power plant turns that into electricity in the steps above. How fast rock heats with depth is in our [geothermal gradient guide](https://zemanium.com/guides/geothermal-gradient), and where the heat lies shallowest is on our [geothermal map](https://zemanium.com/guides/geothermal-map).

02 / Types

## What are the three types of geothermal power plants?

| Type | How it works | Resource | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dry steam | Steam from the reservoir goes straight to the turbine | rare steam-dominated fields | The Geysers, California, producing since 1960; the first geothermal power plant, Italy, 1904 |
| Flash steam | High-pressure hot water drops in pressure at the surface and part of it flashes to steam; the most common type, by the EIA's account | about 200 °C and hotter (NLR baseline) | NLR's representative flash plant: 225 °C water at 2.5 km, 40 MW |
| Binary cycle | Geothermal water heats a second working fluid with a lower boiling point, which drives the turbine in an organic Rankine cycle; the geothermal water never touches the turbine and goes back underground, with no air emissions | about 110 to 200 °C in NLR's baseline; commercial cycles run up to 300 °C | NLR's representative binary plant: 175 °C water at 1.5 km, 30 MW |

Some sites combine flash and binary units. Flash plants produce more power per well because they run hotter, so they need fewer wells for the same output and generally cost less per kilowatt, by NLR's analysis. Binary plants open up the much larger supply of moderate-temperature water.

### What is an organic Rankine cycle?

It is the cycle inside a binary plant. A Rankine cycle boils a fluid, expands the vapour through a turbine, condenses it and pumps it back. Binary plants use a working fluid with a boiling point below that of water, so 110 to 200 °C water can boil it.

03 / Temperature

## Why hotter water makes cheaper power.

The useful work a kilogram of geothermal fluid can deliver rises steeply with its temperature. Our physics model gives the flow exergy, the maximum work available at the wellhead:

| Reservoir temperature | Maximum work per kilogram |
|---|---|
| 200 °C | 172 kJ/kg |
| 300 °C | 354 kJ/kg |
| 400 °C | 566 to 617 kJ/kg |
| 450 °C | 681 to 756 kJ/kg |

[MODEL] · IAPWS water properties · 27 November 2026

Moving from 200 °C to 400 °C more than triples the work per kilogram, so each well powers more of the plant. That is the case for [superhot rock geothermal](https://zemanium.com/guides/superhot-rock-geothermal). How deep that heat lies is in our [geothermal gradient guide](https://zemanium.com/guides/geothermal-gradient), and what the wells cost is in the [wells guide](https://zemanium.com/guides/geothermal-wells).

04 / New plants

## Enhanced and closed-loop geothermal plants.

- Enhanced geothermal systems make a reservoir in hot rock that lacks water or cracks, by opening fractures and circulating water between wells. Fervo Energy's Cape Station in Utah sent first power on 24 September 2026. More in our [enhanced geothermal guide](https://zemanium.com/guides/enhanced-geothermal-systems).
- Closed-loop systems circulate a fluid through sealed wells and take heat by conduction, with no fracturing. Eavor's plant at Geretsried, Germany, has sent power to the grid since December 2025, and its loop circulates by thermosiphon without a pump after start-up, by the company's account. More in our [closed-loop geothermal guide](https://zemanium.com/guides/closed-loop-geothermal).

Both still run a flash or binary plant on the surface. The companies building them are in our [guide to geothermal companies](https://zemanium.com/guides/geothermal-companies).

07 / Questions

## Common questions.

### How does a geothermal power plant generate electricity?

Wells bring hot water or steam from underground. Its heat spins a turbine, directly as steam, as steam flashed from hot water, or through a second fluid in a binary plant. The turbine drives a generator, and the cooled water is injected back underground.

### What are the three types of geothermal power plants?

Dry steam, flash steam and binary cycle. Dry steam plants use steam from the reservoir, flash plants turn high-pressure hot water into steam, and binary plants heat a second fluid with a lower boiling point. Flash plants are the most common, according to the EIA.

### What is a binary cycle power plant?

A geothermal plant that passes the heat of the underground water to a second working fluid, which boils and drives the turbine in an organic Rankine cycle. The geothermal water stays in a closed loop and returns underground. Binary plants suit water of about 110 to 200 °C.

### How much electricity does a geothermal power plant produce?

Plants range from a few megawatts to hundreds. NLR's representative plants are 30 to 50 MW. In Nevada in 2025 the largest single plant code, McGinness Hills 3, produced 562 GWh, and the state's 27 reporting plants produced 5,031 GWh.

### Where is the largest geothermal power plant?

The Geysers in northern California, a complex of 13 plants operated by Calpine with about 725 MW of capacity, is the largest geothermal power complex in the world by Calpine's account.

### Which country produces the most geothermal power?

The United States, with 3,953 MW installed at the end of 2025, followed by Indonesia with 2,742 MW and the Philippines with 2,034 MW, according to ThinkGeoEnergy.

### How efficient is a geothermal power plant?

Geothermal plants convert about 12% of the heat they draw from the ground into electricity on average worldwide, and the best reported, at Darajat in Indonesia, about 21%, by the 2014 worldwide review of Zarrouk and Moon. The heat itself costs nothing, so the efficiency sets how many wells a plant needs.

### Are geothermal power plants bad for the environment?

Their footprint is small. Over their life they emit a median 37 g of CO2-equivalent per kWh, against 486 for natural gas, by NLR's 2021 review, and they scrub hydrogen sulfide and reinject their water. Our guide to the [environmental impacts of geothermal energy](https://zemanium.com/guides/geothermal-environmental-impacts) has the full data.
