# Superhot rock geothermal: what happens above 375 °C.

Superhot rock geothermal means producing heat from rock hotter than about 375 °C at pressures above 22 MPa. Water there is supercritical, and the US Department of Energy says it can deliver several times the power density of cooler rock. Two research wells in Iceland have met fluid hotter than 400 °C, and both were damaged.

Every figure below is quoted from the source listed beside it. Targets are labelled as targets.

01 / In brief

## The short answer.

- The threshold is the critical point of water, about 374 °C and 22.1 MPa. ARPA-E and the US Department of Energy both define superhot as above 375 °C.
- ARPA-E expects a superhot well to produce up to 30 to 50 megawatts of electricity, against about 10 for an enhanced geothermal well today.
- Iceland's IDDP-1 well flowed steam at 450 °C and suffered acid corrosion and silica erosion. IDDP-2 logged 426 °C at about 4,550 m and its casing was damaged.
- Wells fail through their steel, cement and instruments. Casing breaks when the well is heated and cooled, and electronics stop working near 225 °C.

02 / Motive

## Why go hotter.

Each well is expensive, so power per well drives the price of electricity. ARPA-E says current equipment holds enhanced geothermal to about 220 °C and about 10 megawatts of electricity per production well, and that superhot reservoirs could raise this to 30 to 50. Its SUPERHOT program aims at 10 to 20 GW of baseload power at under $30 per megawatt-hour by 2040.

Other estimates of output per well exist. A 2022 study by LucidCatalyst and HERO for the Clean Air Task Force modelled 17 to 29 MW per production well at 55 kg/s. Iceland's power company Orkuveitan is targeting 20 to 50 MW from one well in the IDDP-3 project, and says it has not demonstrated that figure. Conventional wells there make 2 to 7 MW.

03 / Record

## Wells drilled so far.

| Well | What was measured | What went wrong |
|---|---|---|
| IDDP-1, Krafla, Iceland | superheated steam at 450 °C and 140 bar at the wellhead | condensed steam carried hydrochloric and hydrofluoric acid; silica eroded surface equipment when wellhead pressure fell below 80 bar; repeated shutdowns increased corrosion |
| IDDP-2, Reykjanes, Iceland | 426 °C at 340 bar logged at about 4,550 m; about 600 °C inferred at the bottom | production casing damaged between 2,307 and 2,380 m, presumably by cyclic heating and cooling |
| Newberry, Oregon (Mazama Energy) | 331 °C in October 2025; a later well above 700 °F | none reported |

At IDDP-2 the logging tools stayed below 65 °C in rock hotter than 400 to 500 °C for as long as cold water circulated. The production casing was damaged all the same, presumably by repeated heating and cooling.

Next in line: IDDP-3 in Iceland is planned in four stages from late 2026 to 2028. Quaise Energy's Project Obsidian in Oregon targets a well above 400 °C and first power in 2030. Quaise drills with millimetre waves and had completed a 118 m hole in granite by July 2025.

04 / Limits

## What fails first.

| Component | Published limit | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Casing and couplings | most well failures in a review of 20 high-temperature wells came from thermal stress during quenching or the start of production | Kruszewski and Wittig, 2018 |
| Portland cement with 35% to 40% silica flour | proven where produced fluid stays below 350 °C | Kruszewski and Wittig, 2018 |
| Downhole electronics in service | about 210 to 225 °C | Hephae, 2026; IMAPS HiTEC, 2016 |
| Temperature logging tools | 350 °C for four hours | Kruszewski and Wittig, 2018 |
| Polyimide-coated optical fibre | 300 °C continuously, 350 °C briefly | manufacturer's statement (Exail) |
| Silicon carbide circuits, in the laboratory | one year at 500 °C in air | NASA Glenn, 2018 |
| Casing connection test standard ISO/TS 12835:2022 | written for wells that cycle from well below 180 °C up to maxima of 180 °C to 350 °C or above; covers connections only | ISO |

ARPA-E's program description says conventional steel casing and Portland cement are inadequate for superhot wells, and that well materials have to survive high temperature, rapid temperature change and corrosive fluid for 15 years or more.

We rebuilt IDDP-1's production casing in a model and ran it through the well's recorded history: two discharges, then the kill of July 2012. The video log of 2014 found three couplings pulled apart at 300, 356 and 505 m. In 88 of 128 sampled wells our model reproduces that log (SIMULATED, registered and time-stamped before the run). The string parts first at 292 m, just below the point at 290 m where the outer anchor casing changes size, with gaps inside the logged 250 to 400 mm. Two wellhead readings the model was never fitted to land inside their measured bands. The model is calibrated to one well, and the next run is aimed at the second and third partings.

That replay is the test bench for Pandora, our casing joint for wells at 450 to 500 °C. Pandora is built to carry a string through the same history without parting. Its first metal, a subscale joint, is next.

07 / Questions

## Common questions.

### What is superhot rock geothermal?

It is geothermal energy from rock hotter than about 375 °C at pressure above 22 MPa, where water becomes supercritical. A well in those conditions can carry several times the energy of a conventional geothermal well.

### How hot is superhot rock?

Above 375 °C by the definitions of ARPA-E and the US Department of Energy. Iceland's IDDP-1 produced steam at 450 °C, and IDDP-2 logged 426 °C with about 600 °C inferred at the bottom of the well.

### Has anyone produced power from superhot rock?

No commercial plant runs on superhot rock yet. IDDP-1 flowed steam at 450 °C and IDDP-2 logged 426 °C at 340 bar, and both wells were damaged. Quaise Energy targets first power in Oregon in 2030, and Japan's NEDO plans plants around 2040 to 2050.

### What is the IDDP?

The Iceland Deep Drilling Project, a research programme run by Icelandic energy companies to drill into supercritical geothermal zones. IDDP-1 was drilled at Krafla, IDDP-2 at Reykjanes, and IDDP-3 is planned from late 2026.

### Why do superhot wells fail?

Mostly from thermal cycling. Steel casing expands when the well heats and contracts when cold water is pumped in, and the stress breaks casing and couplings. A 2018 review of 20 high-temperature wells found that most failures came from thermal stress during quenching or the start of production.

### How much power can one superhot well produce?

ARPA-E expects up to 30 to 50 megawatts of electricity per well, compared with about 10 megawatts for an enhanced geothermal well at 220 °C. That output has not been demonstrated.
