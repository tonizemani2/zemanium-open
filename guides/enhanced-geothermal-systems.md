# Enhanced geothermal systems: how they work and what limits them.

An enhanced geothermal system (EGS) makes a geothermal reservoir where nature left hot rock without enough water or cracks. Engineers drill into the rock, open fractures in it, and circulate water through them to carry the heat to a power plant.

Figures from companies are their own statements. Figures marked SIMULATED come from Zemanium's models. The field data we show from Utah FORGE was measured and published by others.

01 / In brief

## The short answer.

- Conventional geothermal needs heat, water and permeable rock in the same place. EGS needs only the heat and supplies the other two.
- Fervo Energy's Cape Station in Utah produced first power on 24 September 2026 from a unit of about 33 MW, by the company's account.
- Today's equipment keeps EGS to about 220 °C and about 10 megawatts of electricity per production well, according to ARPA-E.
- The open engineering problems are keeping the reservoir hot for decades, spreading flow evenly through the fractures, building wells that survive, and cost.

02 / Mechanism

## How an enhanced geothermal system works.

1. Drill an injection well into hot, mostly dry rock. Modern projects turn the well horizontal at depth, as shale drillers do.
2. Pump water down under controlled pressure. It opens new fractures and reopens old ones, which gives the rock the permeability it lacked.
3. Drill a production well through the fractured zone, parallel to the first.
4. Circulate water from one well to the other. It heats up as it crosses the fractures.
5. Bring the hot water to the surface, pass its heat to a power plant, and inject the cooled water again.

The water runs in a loop, so the plant delivers power around the clock in any weather. The US Department of Energy also counts two related approaches as next-generation geothermal. Closed-loop systems circulate fluid through sealed pipes in the rock and use no fractures. [Superhot systems](https://zemanium.com/guides/superhot-rock-geothermal) go after rock above 375 °C.

04 / Limits

## Four limits engineers are working on.

### Thermal decline

Injected water is cold. It cools the fracture faces first and the rock behind them more slowly. If most of the flow takes a few short paths, those paths cool within years and the produced temperature drops. How much fracture area the water touches decides how long a well pair lasts. ARPA-E's goal for superhot reservoirs is heat extraction for at least ten years without significant decline.

### Uneven flow

Fractures do not share flow equally. In one published Utah FORGE production log, the interpretation could not resolve how inflow was distributed below 9,240 ft and reported it as a single aggregate. You can [inspect that profile](https://zemanium.com/confidence/#field) in our field replay. The Department of Energy funds work on reversible ways to change fracture permeability for this reason.

### Wells that survive

Casing and cement expand when the well heats and contract when cold water goes down. The Department of Energy says the tools sold for checking cement and casing suit the hot end of oil and gas but are generally inadequate for hotter geothermal wells. Casing and cementing make up 30% to 40% or more of a well's cost.

### Cost

The Department of Energy target is $45 per megawatt-hour by 2035. Our guide to [geothermal electricity cost](https://zemanium.com/guides/geothermal-electricity-cost) shows which inputs move that number.

06 / Questions

## Common questions.

### What does EGS stand for?

Enhanced geothermal system. It is a geothermal reservoir made by engineering: wells are drilled into hot rock, fractures are opened to let water through, and the water carries heat to a power plant.

### How is EGS different from conventional geothermal?

A conventional geothermal plant sits on a natural hydrothermal system, where hot rock, water and permeability already exist together. An enhanced geothermal system starts with hot rock only and adds the water and the permeability, so it can be built in many more places.

### Does enhanced geothermal use fracking?

It uses hydraulic stimulation. Water is injected under controlled pressure to create new fractures and reopen existing ones in hot rock. The aim is permeability for circulating water, and no oil or gas is produced.

### How deep are enhanced geothermal wells?

Current commercial wells are several kilometres long. Fervo Energy's Sawtooth 7 well in Utah reached 19,448 feet of measured depth, about 5.9 km, including a 7,500 foot horizontal section.

### How hot is the rock in an enhanced geothermal system?

Fervo Energy reports producing at about 195 °C and drilling into rock near 238 °C. ARPA-E puts the limit of today's commercial equipment at about 220 °C. Superhot projects target rock above 375 °C.

### How long does an enhanced geothermal reservoir last?

It depends on how much fracture area the circulating water touches. Wide, even sweep keeps the produced temperature up for decades, and concentrated flow cools the rock early. Our economic model assumes 25 years.
